Set the Scene: The Craft of Mise-en-Scène

On intentional framing, and why the eye matters more than the gear

Mise-en-scène is a film term for setting the scene: everything you see in the frame, arranged on purpose. It is where the story is really told, and it is the part of video most people skip.

Mise-en-scène is French for placing on stage. In film it means everything the camera sees, the space, the light, the objects, and the people in it, all arranged with intention. Jared learned it on professional film and television sets, working alongside some of the best in the business, and he brings that same eye to every project.

The story starts before the camera does

Telling the story does not begin when you press record. It begins in the planning: the meetings and decisions in the lead-up to the day, where we work out what the frame should say. Mise-en-scène is that work. It is easy to overlook, and it is often the whole difference between a video that looks fine and one that means something.

What is in the frame: the five parts of mise-en-scène

  • Props: the objects the people on camera use or hold.
  • Costume: what they are wearing.
  • Set: the furniture, the decor, everything else in the room.
  • Blocking: where people stand and how they move.
  • Lighting: the practical lights seen in the shot, and the lights we bring to shape the scene and the people in it.

Each one is a choice, and each choice tells the audience something before anyone says a word.

Show, don't tell

"Show, don't tell" is the oldest advice in filmmaking, and mise-en-scène is how you do it. You show the story through the frame. How a person is framed, and the setting they are in, tells the audience how to feel about what they are saying, often with far more impact than the words alone. A well-lit scene with no life in it will never land the way a scene that has been thought through does.

The eye matters more than the gear

A camera is a tool, and a list of tools tells you nothing about whether someone can tell a story. The gear is the least important part of the work. Knowing how to use it, and having the eye to arrange what it points at, leaves a far greater impact on an audience than owning the most expensive equipment.

Why it makes people better on camera

Most people freeze the moment a lens points at them. Mise-en-scène quietly fixes that. Put someone in a setting that feels natural to them, and give them something real to do, and they stop performing. They forget the camera is there and simply have a conversation, and that is when they come across as themselves: natural, sincere, and worth watching.

FAQ

What is mise-en-scène?

A film term, French for setting the scene. It refers to everything you see in the frame, the space, the lighting, the props, the costume, and the people in it, all arranged on purpose to tell the story.

Why does mise-en-scène matter for a business video?

Because the frame tells the story before a word is spoken. Getting the scene right is what separates a video that looks fine from one that actually lands.

Does better video just mean better gear?

No. Gear is only as good as the person using it. Framing and lighting do the heavy lifting of telling a story, which is why a filmmaker with a phone will beat a person with an expensive camera.

How do you make someone comfortable on camera?

We put them in a setting that feels natural and give them something real to do, so they forget the lens and simply have a conversation.

When does the mise-en-scène work happen?

Before the camera rolls. It is part of the planning and the production meetings in the lead-up to the day, not an afterthought on the day itself.
